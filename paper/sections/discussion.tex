\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the experiments support}
We started from four gaps (Section~\ref{sec:intro}) and proposed a pipeline that couples segmentation, evidence extraction, ordinal grading, rule consistency and conformal referral. The experiments give a mixed but informative picture, which we summarise by component rather than by headline.

\paragraph{Lesion evidence as input to the grader: supported} The clearest result is that lesion-derived statistics carry grading information that a generic image embedding does not capture. A 120-number summary of the segmentation output (B3) is as informative about DR grade as a 1152-dimensional ImageNet embedding (QWK 0.641 vs.\ 0.638--0.644), and the two are complementary (QWK 0.72--0.73 together, AUC for referable DR 0.92 versus 0.87). The mechanism is plausible and, in our setting, partly an effect of \emph{supervision}: the segmenter is trained on 383 images with pixel-level labels, so the evidence tensor imports expert lesion annotation that the grade labels of 6260 images and a frozen ImageNet backbone cannot provide. We did not isolate this by, for example, training the segmenter with fewer annotated images, so we cannot say how the gain scales with the number of pixel-annotated images; this would be informative for deciding how much annotation effort is justified. That the mild and severe grades remain poorly recognised shows that the coarse lesion statistics are not sufficient for the finest clinical distinctions (a few microaneurysms; the 4--2--1 rule requires venous beading and IRMA, which are not annotated and not segmented).

\paragraph{Token architecture versus naive fusion: marginal} The transformer head over evidence tokens is only slightly better than concatenation (+0.013 QWK, interval just excluding zero). We would not build a case for the architecture on accuracy. Its justification is that the attention over $(c,z)$ tokens gives a direct view of which lesion class and zone matter for a prediction, which a concatenated MLP does not. We did not evaluate this interpretability quantitatively (for example with a deletion test or with ophthalmologist review), so it remains a design argument rather than a result.

\paragraph{Soft ordinal targets: a calibration effect} Gaussian-smoothed targets reduced the ECE from 0.095 to 0.058 without changing agreement. Since calibration is a precondition for any threshold-based referral or conformal procedure, this is useful, but the effect is on confidence rather than on decisions.

\paragraph{Clinical-rule consistency: not demonstrated} The rule loss did not reduce rule violations in a meaningful way and did not improve accuracy. Our own analysis points to the reason: the data-calibrated necessary conditions are rarely violated by any of the models. We consider this a legitimate negative finding. It does not show that clinical knowledge is useless for DR grading, but it shows that this form of knowledge (soft necessary conditions calibrated on the same labels, with quadrants instead of ETDRS fields, and without the lesions that define severe disease) adds little beyond what the evidence already conveys. The \emph{violation rate} itself remains, we believe, a useful audit measure, and it would become more discriminating with stricter rules.

\paragraph{Conformal referral: valid construction, invalid assumption on DDR partitions} Conformal sets achieved their nominal coverage when calibration and test data were exchangeable and fell short when the calibration set came from the official validation partition. The 8.5-point accuracy difference between the DDR validation and test partitions (84\% vs.\ 75\%) is a finding in its own right: results tuned on the DDR validation set will be optimistic relative to the test set for \emph{any} method, and conformal calibration inherits the optimism. Practitioners should calibrate on data from the deployment distribution, and benchmark papers should report validation-test gaps.

\paragraph{External generalisation: not achieved} The zero-shot transfer to the Gaussian-filtered Kaggle images failed for all models. Because the external images are $224\times224$ and filtered, the experiment conflates population shift with a resolution mismatch that no method based on tiny-lesion evidence could overcome. It does, however, show that the pipeline as built is tied to the DDR acquisition setting, and it argues against reading the DDR results as evidence of cross-population robustness.

\subsection{Relation to prior work}
Our results are consistent with the premise of lesion-aware grading approaches \citep{wang2017zoomin,yang2017twostage,sun2021lat,huang2021lesioncl}, namely that lesion information helps grading and that image-level labels alone leave this information underused. We do not compare numerically with their published results, because splits, resolutions, backbones and compute differ and we have not re-run their code; absolute numbers from a frozen-backbone, CPU-trained study should not be compared with GPU-trained end-to-end systems. The distinct elements of this study --- zone-wise soft evidence as the only route from lesions to grade, rule-based consistency checking, and conformal referral analysed under both exchangeable and shifted calibration --- are, to our knowledge, not covered by earlier DR work, though we cannot rule out that related ideas exist in publications that we did not find; the literature comparison in Table~\ref{tab:lit} is qualitative and based on our reading.

\subsection{Clinical interpretation}
At the level of a screening programme, the most relevant outputs are the referable-DR sensitivity and specificity. With a hard arg-max decision the lesion-grounded model detects 68\% of referable cases at 95\% specificity on this test partition. Conformal referral with $\alpha=0.1$ raises sensitivity to 0.85 under exchangeability at 0.88 specificity, but, because sensitivity of a screening tool should be as high as possible and the partition-shift result shows how fragile such operating points are, any deployment would need local calibration. The poor recognition of mild DR (grade~1) matters less for referral (mild DR is not referable) than the confusion of moderate with normal (37\% of moderate cases), which is where missed referrals arise. We caution against any clinical reading of these retrospective results.
